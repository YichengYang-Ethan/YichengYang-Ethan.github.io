What Prediction Markets Got Right (and Wrong) During the Final Four

Yicheng Yang · April 5, 2026 · 5 min read

Illinois made the Final Four for the first time since 2005. If you were anywhere near campus last night, you know what that means — watch parties everywhere, the bars on Green Street packed before tipoff, the whole town locked in. I was in there with a few thousand other students, losing my mind like everyone else when we briefly took the lead in the first half.

But in the background, a script on my laptop was quietly polling the Kalshi and Polymarket APIs every 15–30 seconds. The goal: test whether the pricing wedge I estimated in my main paper — across 291,309 contracts — actually exists in real time, tick by tick, during a single game's two-hour lifecycle.

Short answer: it does. And the magnitude matches. (The game itself, unfortunately, did not go our way.)

The Data

My collector ran from 3:51 PM to 7:38 PM CT on April 4, covering the full Illinois–UConn semifinal. It captured 657 Kalshi and 857 Polymarket observations on the game-winner contract, plus ESPN's play-by-play (439 plays, 74 scoring events) for a score-based benchmark.

The Price Trajectory

Both platforms opened Illinois at roughly 55%, consistent with sportsbook consensus. We held a narrow 22–21 lead midway through the first half — that was the moment the room was loudest. But Illinois never led again after the 5:02 mark. UConn's second-half surge, building a lead of up to 14, was painful to watch in person but fascinating to watch on the price chart. By 7:16 PM, both platforms had Illinois below 5%.

That cross-platform spread is itself interesting. Kalshi's $0.01 minimum tick constrains price resolution at the extremes, and the two user bases clearly process game information at different speeds. Sustained spreads of 10–15 percentage points during active play suggest cross-platform arbitrage in live sports markets remains limited, even for a game this high-profile.

The Pricing Wedge Test

My main paper estimates that prediction markets systematically overprice contracts by about λ = 0.183 — roughly speaking, an event with a 10% true probability trades around 13.5%. But that estimate comes from averaging across hundreds of thousands of contracts. Does this overpricing exist in real time, during a single game?

To test this, I built a simple score-based win probability model (it correlates at r = 0.982 with ESPN's proprietary model) and computed the Wang λ at every market tick. The naive answer looked dramatic: λ = +0.57, three times the cross-sectional estimate.

That number is wrong. Here's why.

Once UConn led by 14 points, the score model assigned Illinois a 2–3% chance of winning, while the market still priced them at 5–8% (Kalshi can't price below $0.01). The math I use to compute λ — the probit transform Φ−1 — has a slope that goes to infinity near 0 and 1. So a modest 3-percentage-point gap in the tail gets amplified into a massive λ. The “finding” was an artifact.

Filter to the competitive range — the period when the game was genuinely uncertain (p* ∈ [0.35, 0.55], roughly the first 30 game-minutes) — and the real number emerges: λ ≈ +0.13 on both platforms. That's within one standard error of the cross-sectional estimate of 0.183 from the main paper.

The pricing wedge is not a cross-sectional artifact. It is embedded in real-time market prices at the tick level. At competitive probabilities, the intra-game λ of +0.13 matches the estimate from 291,309 contracts.

Kalshi vs Polymarket

Against ESPN's own in-game model (a sharper benchmark than my score model), Kalshi showed essentially zero pricing error — λ = +0.01, not statistically significant (p = 0.06). Polymarket left a small but significant residual: λ = +0.08 (p < 0.001).

The interpretation is straightforward. Kalshi's regulated market, populated by U.S. participants, priced this game as accurately as ESPN's proprietary model. Polymarket's crypto-native user base — different demographics, different information sources — left money on the table. For this game, at least, the regulated platform was the sharper market.

What This Means

This is a single game. I can't draw sweeping conclusions from N = 1. But the fact that the real-time pricing wedge matches the cross-sectional estimate from 291,309 contracts is reassuring: the framework isn't just a statistical artifact of averaging. The pricing wedge is baked into every tick.

The methodological lesson matters too. Anyone applying the Wang Transform to live sports data needs to filter extreme probabilities. The probit transform inflates λ by a factor of four or more in the tails. Report the competitive-range estimate, not the full-sample number.

It stings that Illinois didn't pull it off — this team deserved a better ending. But Monday night, the championship game tips off, and the collector is already running. Maybe next year we'll be the ones on the right side of the price chart.

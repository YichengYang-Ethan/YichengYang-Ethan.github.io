Where streaming hooks into PyMC's ELBO machinery

Yicheng Yang · May 2026 · ← All GSoC notes

Variational inference works great until your data doesn't fit in RAM. My summer 2026 GSoC project with NumFOCUS / PyMC is fixing that — making ADVI and Pathfinder work on datasets that don't fit in memory. Mentors are Rob Zinkov (@zaxtax) and Chris Fonnesbeck (@fonnesbeck).

Week 1 was mostly orientation, but with two concrete artifacts: a working prototype and a design draft, ready for the May 26 design review with Rob.

What I didn't expect

Minibatch variational inference relies on a simple identity: if you compute the expected log-likelihood over a random subset of size |B| out of N total data points and multiply by N/|B|, you get an unbiased estimate of the full-data ELBO. That's the trick that makes VI scale to large datasets.

What I didn't realize until I read the source: in PyMC 6, those two concerns — randomly sampling a batch, and applying the N/|B| scaling factor in the log-probability computation — live in two different files. pm.Minibatch in pymc/data.py:121 does the row sampling. MinibatchRandomVariable in pymc/variational/minibatch_rv.py:102-106 applies the scaling when the log-prob is registered: 

# pymc/variational/minibatch_rv.py — where the N/|B| scaling actually lives
@_logprob.register(MinibatchRandomVariable)
def minibatch_rv_logprob(op, values, *inputs, **kwargs):
    [value] = values
    rv, *total_size = inputs
    return logp(rv, value, **kwargs) * get_scaling(total_size, value.shape)

That decoupling is why a 12-week project is actually tractable. The scaling machinery is already there, working correctly. StreamingDataset only has to replace the data source — feed a pytensor.shared buffer instead of a numpy array — and the existing minibatch RV handles the rest.

What I'm sketching

StreamingDataset wraps a pytensor.shared buffer of fixed shape (batch_size, *features). An advance() method pulls the next batch from any Python iterable — a generator, a file reader, a network stream — and writes it into the shared buffer. A fit_callback() method gives pm.fit something to call after each gradient step so the buffer is refreshed for the next iteration.

To verify the algorithm is preserved: ran a synthetic test on N = 50,000 data points. Streaming ADVI (256-row batches, never holding more than 256 rows in memory) converges to within 0.003 of in-memory ADVI's posterior mean — comfortably inside ADVI's own optimization noise. The point isn't precision; it's that the streaming version is provably the same algorithm, just with a different data path.

Also this week — first pymc-examples PR

Opened my first pymc-examples PR (#882) this week — a PyMC 6 / ArviZ 1 compatibility fix for variational_api_quickstart.ipynb, the official VI tutorial notebook. Started as four small fixes: pm.callbacks had moved, az.plot_posterior was removed, Approximation.sample() now needs a model context, total_size wants an int instead of a tuple.

What I didn't expect was the review. Osvaldo Martin (@aloctavodia, one of ArviZ's core maintainers) came back with eight specific suggestions — not just bug fixes, but a migration to the new ArviZ 1 plot API: az.plot_dist({"NUTS": idata, "ADVI": idata}) dict form for multi-model comparisons, az.plot_trace_dist instead of plot_trace, az.convert_to_dataset for wrapping raw numpy, pc.add_legend("model"). Three rounds of commits later, 18 review comments addressed.

Two takeaways. ArviZ has a richer visualization grammar than I realized, and I'll be using it when reporting streaming ADVI results later in the project. And doing a small focused contribution alongside the design phase is a low-cost way to learn the project's contribution culture before submitting anything bigger.
